EXECUTIVE SUMMARY / ABSTRACT

Accurate estimation of residential rental prices is a pivotal machine learning application with substantial socio-economic impact. Rental real estate markets are notoriously heterogeneous, characterized by acute spatial dependencies, non-linear feature interactions, and heavy right-skewed price distributions. In this study, an end-to-end Machine Learning regression framework was developed to predict monthly residential rents across six major metropolitan hubs in India (Mumbai, Bangalore, Chennai, Hyderabad, Delhi, and Kolkata).

The raw dataset comprising 4,746 listings and 12 baseline attributes was cleaned, preprocessed, and enriched through domain-driven feature engineering, including floor hierarchy decomposition and spatial density ratios. To mitigate extreme heteroscedasticity and outlier dominance, a natural logarithmic target transformation was applied. Five distinct machine learning algorithms representing diverse inductive paradigms were trained and evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation: Multiple Linear Regression (OLS), Ridge Regression ($L_2$ regularization), Decision Tree Regressor (CART), Random Forest Regressor (Bagging Ensemble), and Gradient Boosting Regressor (Sequential Boosting).

The empirical benchmark revealed that ensemble architectures substantially outperformed linear baselines. The Gradient Boosting Regressor achieved superior generalization with a test-set Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of ₹10,795, a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 29.46%, and a log-scale $R^2$ score of 0.8428 (5-Fold CV $R^2 = 0.8255 \pm 0.0102$). Feature importance analysis confirmed that property size (sq. ft.), metropolitan city tier (specifically Mumbai's spatial premium), and bathroom count constitute the primary drivers of rental valuation. Finally, the optimized pipeline was deployed as an interactive, production-ready Streamlit web application enabling real-time valuation, confidence interval estimation, and comparative market diagnostics.

1. PROBLEM STATEMENT & REAL-WORLD MOTIVATION

1.1 Problem Statement

Given a set of structured physical, spatial, and contractual property characteristics $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the objective is to learn a predictive mapping function $f: \mathbf{x} \to y$, where $y \in \mathbb{R}^+$ denotes the monthly house rent in Indian Rupees (INR). This constitutes a continuous multi-variable supervised regression problem.

1.2 Real-World Motivation

The residential rental market across urban India has witnessed exponential growth due to rapid urbanization, employment migration to tech corridors, and changing demographics. However, the market suffers from: 1. Information Asymmetry: Tenants often overpay due to opaque rental pricing models dictated by intermediaries. 2. Sub-optimal Landlord Pricing: Property owners frequently experience high vacancy rates due to unrealistic rent expectations, or suffer revenue loss from under-pricing. 3. Extreme Geographic Disparities: A 2-BHK apartment in Mumbai commands vastly different rental economics compared to an identical unit in Kolkata or Hyderabad due to local land availability constraints.

Developing an algorithmic pricing system provides fair-market transparency, stabilizes rental yield expectations, and automates real estate valuation.

3. DATA PREPROCESSING & FEATURE ENGINEERING

3.1 Floor Attribute Decomposition

The raw Floor column contained composite alphanumeric strings (e.g., "Ground out of 2", "3 out of 10", "Upper Basement out of 4"). Regular expressions were applied to split this into two discrete numerical variables: 1. Floor_Level: Numerical level (Basements mapped to negative values: Upper Basement $\to -1$, Lower Basement $\to -2$; Ground $\to 0$; $n$-th floor $\to n$). 2. Total_Floors: Total vertical floors in the residential tower.

3.2 Domain-Specific Ratio Features

To capture architectural efficiency and lifestyle quality, four domain-specific features were engineered: - $\text{Size per BHK} = \frac{\text{Size}}{\text{BHK}}$: Measures average living space per bedroom. - $\text{Bathroom to BHK Ratio} = \frac{\text{Bathroom}}{\text{BHK}}$: Indicates luxury-tier sanitary convenience. - $\text{Is Ground Floor} \in \{0, 1\}$: Binary indicator ($1$ if Floor_Level == $0$). - $\text{Is Top Floor} \in \{0, 1\}$: Binary indicator ($1$ if Floor_Level $\ge$ Total_Floors).

3.3 Feature Transformation & Scaling Pipeline

To prevent data leakage, preprocessing was encapsulated within a strict Scikit-Learn ColumnTransformer: - Continuous Numerical Features (BHK, Size, Bathroom, Floor_Level, Total_Floors, Size_per_BHK, etc.): Standardized using StandardScaler to have zero mean and unit variance: $$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$ - Categorical Features (City, Furnishing Status, Tenant Preferred, Area Type, Point of Contact): Encoded using OneHotEncoder(drop='first', sparse_output=False, handle_unknown='ignore') to avoid dummy variable collinearity. - Data Partitioning: Partitioned into 80% Training ($N = 3,796$) and 20% Testing ($N = 950$) using stratified random seeding (random_state=42).

4.1 Multiple Linear Regression (OLS Baseline)

Serves as the empirical benchmark. It models the relationship as an affine linear combination: $$\hat{y}_{\log} = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j x_j$$ Estimated via minimizing the sum of squared residuals: $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{OLS}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^T \boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^2$$

4.2 Ridge Regression ($L_2$ Regularization)

Introduces a Tikhonov $L_2$ penalty on the coefficient magnitudes to prevent multicollinearity among correlated spatial and physical predictors: $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^T \boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^2 + \alpha \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$$ Regularization parameter was calibrated to $\alpha = 1.0$.

4.3 Decision Tree Regressor (CART)

A non-parametric baseline that recursively partitions the predictor space into $M$ orthogonal hyper-rectangles $R_1, \dots, R_M$. At each node, the split $(j, s)$ is chosen to minimize squared error variance: $$\min_{j, s} \left[ \sum_{i \in R_1(j, s)} (y_i - \bar{y}_{R_1})^2 + \sum_{i \in R_2(j, s)} (y_i - \bar{y}_{R_2})^2 \right]$$ Hyperparameters: max_depth=8, min_samples_split=10 to avoid memorization.

4.4 Random Forest Regressor (Bagging)

An ensemble of $B = 150$ de-correlated decision trees trained via bootstrap aggregating (bagging) with random feature subspace sampling at each split: $$\hat{f}_{\text{RF}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B T_b(\mathbf{x})$$ Random Forests effectively reduce variance without increasing bias, making them resilient to noise and localized price fluctuations.

4.5 Gradient Boosting Regressor (Boosting)

Constructs an additive ensemble of shallow regression trees sequentially. At each iteration $m$, a new decision tree $h_m(\mathbf{x})$ is fitted to the negative gradient (pseudo-residuals) of the squared-error loss function: $$r_{im} = -\left[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(y_i, f(\mathbf{x}_i))}{\partial f(\mathbf{x}_i)} \right]_{f = f_{m-1}} = y_i - f_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)$$ The model updates via shrinkage: $$f_m(\mathbf{x}) = f_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta \cdot h_m(\mathbf{x})$$ Hyperparameters: $M = 180$ estimators, learning rate $\eta = 0.07$, max_depth=5, and subsample=0.85.

5. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP & EVALUATION METRICS

5.1 Evaluation Metrics Formulation

Models were evaluated on both the transformed log-space and the original currency space (INR) using four standard performance metrics:
  1. Mean Absolute Error (MAE): Represents the average absolute error magnitude in Indian Rupees:
  2. $$\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$$
  3. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): Penalizes disproportionately large prediction deviations:
  4. $$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}$$
  5. Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE): Measures relative percentage deviation:
  6. $$\text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left| \frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{y_i} \right|$$
  7. Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$ Score): Quantifies the proportion of target variance explained:
  8. $$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$

6.2 Key Analytical Insights

  1. Superiority of Ensemble Learning:
  2. - Gradient Boosting achieved the highest overall explanatory power, attaining a cross-validated $R^2$ of 0.8255 and test-set log $R^2$ of 0.8428. It minimized relative error to 29.46% MAPE.
  3. - Random Forest achieved the lowest raw rupee error with a test-set MAE of ₹10,609.37, demonstrating the variance-reduction capability of bagging.
  4. Impact of Log Transformation on Error Boundaries:
  5. - When models were trained directly on unscaled target rents, the presence of multi-million-rupee luxury listings caused Linear Regression and Gradient Boosting to incur an MAE exceeding ₹21,700. By transforming to $\ln(1 + \text{Rent})$, the MAE was cut in half down to ₹10,600 – ₹10,800, confirming that rental pricing errors scale proportionally rather than additively.
  6. Linear vs. Regularized Ridge:
  7. - Linear Regression and Ridge Regression exhibited virtually identical metrics (MAE difference $< ₹2.00$). This indicates that our engineered feature set does not suffer from ill-conditioned collinearity, validating the pre-screening of correlated columns.
  8. Instability of Single Decision Trees:
  9. - The single Decision Tree regressor underperformed all other methods, with an RMSE of ₹43,431 and an $R^2$ of 0.5267. Its axis-aligned, step-wise partitioning cannot smoothly capture continuous real estate appreciation gradients.

9. CHALLENGES, LIMITATIONS & FUTURE SCOPE

9.1 Technical Challenges Encountered

1. Target Skewness: Raw rent values spanning four orders of magnitude (₹1,200 to ₹3,500,000) initially distorted mean squared error calculations. Resolved via natural log transformation. 2. Floor String Parsing: Floor data was captured in unstructured string formats ("Upper Basement out of 4", "Ground out of 1"). Implemented regular expression parsers to convert textual floor levels into continuous signed integers.

9.2 Limitations

- Locality Resolution: High cardinality of individual neighborhood names (over 2,200 unique localities) precluded direct one-hot encoding without dimensionality explosion. - Omission of Temporal Market Shifts: The dataset represents a snapshot from 2022; inflation and post-pandemic office return trends are not modeled.

9.3 Future Directions

- Spatial Embeddings: Incorporating latitude and longitude coordinates with Geo-Spatial Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) or spatial k-nearest neighbor distance features. - Image Integration (Multimodal ML): Incorporating interior property images alongside tabular data using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to evaluate aesthetic appeal and renovation quality.
